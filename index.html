SHEET 01 / 04

Flow Channel Expansion Joint

SPONSOR — CLOACINA
CAL POLY SLO, 2025–2026
ROLE — RUBBER COMPONENT ENGINEER
Photos and detailed FEA/GD&T documentation for this project haven't been added yet — send them over and I'll build out this page the same way as the wind turbine sheet.

Sole rubber-component engineer on a 4-person senior capstone team.

Cloacina's modular wastewater system needed an open U-channel that could absorb 1 inch of deflection in any direction without failing or leaking. I owned the rubber component from concept through delivery: building a nonlinear FEA model in ANSYS to predict how the rubber would deform and where it would fail, then running physical prototypes to check that prediction against reality.

The two didn't agree at first. I spent a good part of the project tracing the gap between what the model said and what the prototypes actually did, adjusting both the geometry and the model until they converged. The final design was documented with full GD&T detail drawings and a manufacturing plan, and delivered as a working prototype on schedule.

SHEET 02 / 04

Wind Turbine Design, Build & Compete

ME 488 ROTOR COMPETITION, CAL POLY SLO
JUNE 2026
TEAM — LIAM MCGEE, JAMES MONTELINDO, ANDEN HOLLEY
FIG 2.1 — Assembled three-blade NACA 4412 rotor (click to enlarge)

Aerodynamic analysis (QBlade), structural assembly, and blade validation.

For the ME 488 Wind Turbine Rotor Competition, our three-person team designed, fabricated, and tested a three-bladed NACA 4412 rotor. The design targeted a tip-speed ratio of six and a baseline wind speed of 13 mph, chosen from real interval wind data at the test site as a conservative-but-capable design point.

We picked the NACA 4412 airfoil specifically because it's cambered with high lift at modest angles of attack — well suited to the low-to-moderate Reynolds numbers a small turbine operates at — and its 12% thickness gave the blade enough structural resistance to bending and vibration while still being manufacturable. James led the core CAD model in SolidWorks; I ran the blade through QBlade independently to cross-check our BEM spreadsheet results against professional software before we committed to fabrication.

Three blades was a deliberate trade-off: added blades give diminishing returns on power once you're past three, and with only a week to fully manufacture the rotor after finalizing the design, minimizing print time mattered as much as aerodynamics.

Liam and I handled structural assembly — inserting steel reinforcement rods between the 3D-printed blade sections, epoxying the joints, and validating blade strength before competition day.

Selected sections from a 43-section Blade Element Momentum theory analysis, used to loft the SolidWorks blade model. Full spreadsheet included angle of attack (5°), C_l = 1, C_d = 0.008 at that operating point, sourced from the NACA 4412 airfoil polar.

Results

Using a conservative site wind speed of 3.3 m/s, our design's theoretical maximum power was 25 W with a theoretical coefficient of power of 0.53. On competition day, the rotor produced 9 W over the 10-minute test, for a measured C_p of 0.36 — a solid result for a first design iteration, finishing a close second out of all competing teams. The gap between theoretical and measured C_p is consistent with estimation error in wind speed and airfoil C_l/C_d at the real operating Reynolds number, not a flaw in the underlying method.

SHEET 03 / 04

Campus Water Heating System

CAL POLY SLO, SAN LUIS OBISPO
ROLE — THERMAL SYSTEMS DESIGN
Photos and the full EES model/report for this project haven't been added yet — send them over and I'll build out this page the same way as the wind turbine sheet.

Individual thermal systems design project.

The brief: design a cost-optimized hot-water system for the ground floor of the university's largest building. I modeled the system using 1D transient and steady-state nodal network analysis, treating the piping network as a set of interconnected thermal nodes rather than guessing at a single lumped estimate.

Pipe diameters, layout, pump selection, and heat exchanger sizing all came out of an iterative EES model, cross-checked against real product data and cost estimation software, so the final recommendation was justified on both performance and cost — not just whichever component looked best on paper.

Individual controls project, system identification and PID design.

Given only measured input/output data from a dynamic system, the task was to work backward to a usable model. I used MATLAB system identification techniques to extract a state-space representation directly from the data, rather than deriving it from first principles.

From there I tuned a PID controller against that identified model and validated the closed-loop response against defined performance specifications — overshoot, settling time, steady-state error. It's a small project, but it's the same instinct that ran through my senior project: don't trust a model until you've checked it against something measured.
